Byzantine Monitors in AI Control: A Threshold Hierarchy for Budgeted Oversight

J. Suh et al.
Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research (JAIR)  ·  In preparation, 2026

A common proposal for supervising an untrusted AI is to assign a committee of monitors to oversee it: the monitors flag anything suspicious, and a scarce, trusted auditor examines whatever gets flagged. This paper asks what happens when some of those monitors are themselves corrupt, and are quietly working with the agent they are meant to be policing to hide a single act of sabotage.

Within the settings of the paper, the corrupt monitors have no channel to talk to each other while the system runs, and no way to pay each other off. They coordinate only through a shared secret agreed in advance and a hidden signal the agent buries in its own visible output. This setup rules out the classical results on collusion-proof mechanisms, and turns the question into one concerning worst-case correlation under a fixed audit budget.

Safety turns out to be governed by the fraction of corrupt monitors through three closed-form thresholds, written in terms of the monitors’ true- and false-positive rates. Scoring each action made by the AI agent independently stays safe only below the lowest of them; past it, it does no better than auditing at random. Looking instead at how flags correlate across actions does provably better, and the paper gives an explicit polynomial-time overseer that restores safety up to a strictly higher threshold, because hiding sabotage still forces the colluders to coordinate visibly on the decoys used to bury it. The third threshold is a universal ceiling that no overseer can beat.
